How it works, step by step

What happens between clicking "Upload" and seeing the report. It takes a few seconds.

  1. Read the PDF

    The server pulls the plain text out of the PDF. Scanned photos of a resume have no real text, so the system says so instead of guessing.

  2. Find the skills

    It looks for 114 known skills such as Python, React, SQL or Figma, and understands common variations, so "NodeJS" counts as Node.js and "K8s" as Kubernetes. Everyday words like "react quickly" are not mistaken for skills.

  3. Predict the career

    A machine learning model compares the resume's words with 13,587 examples it has learned from and gives a probability for each of the 25 careers. The highest one is the prediction, and the top three are shown with their percentages.

  4. Score the resume and find gaps

    Each career has six core skills. The resume score is the share of those skills the resume already shows, and the missing ones become the skill gaps.

  5. Recommend what to do next

    It suggests courses and likely interview questions for that career, plus open jobs on the platform ranked by how many of their required skills the person already has.

How the test was done

The real CVs were split into two groups before training. The model learned from 80% of them. The other 20%, which is 2,603 CVs, were hidden until the end and used only to check its answers. Because it had never seen those resumes, the score reflects how it will do on new people.

How each score is calculated

Every score is a simple, explainable formula. Nothing is hidden.

Resume score

How many of the predicted career's six core skills the resume shows.

resume score = skills found ÷ 6 × 100

Example: a Backend Developer resume showing 5 of the 6 core skills scores 83%.

Job match (when a job description is pasted)

How similar the words in the resume and the job post are, after removing common words like "the" and "and". Rare, specific words such as "Laravel" count more than everyday ones.

job match = text similarity × 100

Applicant ranking (what employers see)

Each applicant is scored right after they apply, using the resume they attached.

match score = 70% skill overlap + 30% text similarity

Skills carry most of the weight because they are the most objective signal. The text part rewards relevant experience and a tailored cover letter. If the AI service is ever down, it falls back to skill overlap alone so ranking still works.

Job recommendations for seekers

Every open job is checked against the skills on the person's latest resume.

job score = required skills they have ÷ required skills × 100
+ 20 bonus when the job title matches their predicted career

How the real CVs were turned into training data

  1. Download

    The English CV file (237 MB, 210,250 CVs) was downloaded from Hugging Face.

  2. Map each CV to a career

    Each CV has a role keyword (such as "JavaScript" or "Data Science") and a job title. Clear rules turn these into one of our careers. For example, a JavaScript CV titled "Full Stack Developer" becomes Full Stack Developer, and a Data Science CV titled "NLP Engineer" becomes NLP Engineer. CVs for roles outside our 25 careers, such as QA testers, HR or sales, are skipped.

  3. Balance the careers

    At most 700 CVs are kept per career, picked at random (reservoir sampling, seed 42), so huge groups like JavaScript developers don't drown out small ones.

  4. Clean the text

    The job title and CV text are joined, extra spaces are removed, the text is cut to 2,000 characters, and CVs shorter than 120 characters are dropped.

2. The career model

Step 1, turn text into numbers (TF-IDF). For each word or two-word phrase t in a resume d:

tf(t, d) = 1 + ln(count of t in d)
idf(t) = ln((1 + N) ÷ (1 + df(t))) + 1
weight(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t), then the whole vector is scaled to length 1

N is the number of training texts and df(t) is how many of them contain t. Common words get a low weight, specific ones a high weight.

Step 2, score each career (Logistic Regression). The model has a weight for every feature and career:

score_k = w_k · x + b_k   (for each of the 25 careers k)
P(career k) = e^score_k ÷ Σ e^score_j   (softmax, all 25 add up to 100%)

Step 3, pick. The highest probability is the prediction. The top three are shown with their percentages.

Balanced class weight for career k:

weight_k = total rows ÷ (25 × rows of career k)

How accuracy is measured:

accuracy = correct predictions ÷ all predictions
precision = correct as k ÷ all predicted as k
recall = correct as k ÷ all that really are k
F1 = 2 × precision × recall ÷ (precision + recall)
macro F1 = average F1 over all careers
top-3 accuracy = right career is among the 3 highest probabilities

Curated examples: every way of choosing 4 of a career's 6 core skills, C(6, 4) = 15, × 25 careers = 375 rows.

About the AI
Is this real artificial intelligence?

Yes. The career prediction is a machine learning model that learned patterns from 13,587 examples, including 13,012 real CVs. Nobody wrote rules like "if Python then Data Scientist". It worked out which words point to which career by itself.

What kind of model is it?

A Logistic Regression classifier on TF-IDF text features. In plain words: the resume is turned into a list of word weights (rare, specific words weigh more), and the model has learned how strongly each word points to each of the 25 careers. It adds these up and turns them into percentages.

Why not use ChatGPT or a large deep learning model?

Three reasons. It runs entirely on our own server, so resumes are never sent to an outside company. It is fast and needs no GPU. And it was tested: a more complex model (a linear SVM) scored 86.2%, only 0.1 points higher, so the extra complexity was not worth it.

Does it use ChatGPT or any outside AI service?

No. All the AI runs inside the project's own AI service. The only outside calls are to public job boards when an admin imports remote jobs.

Where did the training data come from?

Mostly from the Djinni Recruitment Dataset, a public collection of 210,000 anonymised CVs from an IT job platform, released for research under the MIT licence and published at the LREC-COLING 2024 conference. We mapped 13,012 of them to our 25 careers. It is combined with 375 curated examples and 200 rows from an earlier Kaggle dataset.

Can the model be improved further?

Yes. The biggest gains would come from more real CVs for the non-tech careers and the smaller ones like Research Scientist. Adding data and re-running one training command rebuilds the model and prints its new accuracy automatically.

Accuracy and trust
How accurate is it, in one sentence?

It picks the exact right career 86 times out of 100, and the right career is in its top three 98 times out of 100, measured on real CVs it had never seen.

How do we know the 86% is not inflated?

Because the 2,603 test CVs were separated before training and never shown to the model while it learned. Testing on data the model has already seen would give a falsely high number. This was not done here.

What happens when it is unsure?

It shows its top three careers with percentages, so a close call is visible. For example a mixed resume might show 86% Full Stack and 4% Data Scientist. If a resume has very little text, it refuses to guess and asks for a proper PDF instead.

Could the AI be biased?

It only reads resume text and skills. It does not use names, gender, age, photos or location to predict a career or rank applicants. Bias can still come from how people describe their work, which is why the scores are advice only and a human makes every hiring decision.

Does the AI decide who gets hired?

No. It sorts applicants and explains why (matched and missing skills), but every status change, from shortlisted to selected, is made by a person at the employer.

What kinds of resumes does it struggle with?

Scanned image PDFs (no readable text), resumes for non-tech jobs outside the 25 careers, and resumes that list very few specific skills. It also mixes up careers that genuinely overlap, such as Marketing Manager and Digital Marketer.

Scores and results
Why is my resume score low if my resume is good?

The resume score only measures how many of the six core skills for the predicted career your resume mentions. It does not judge writing quality or experience. Adding the missing skills you genuinely have will raise it.

What is the difference between the ML and rule-based career?

The ML career comes from the trained model reading the whole resume, and it drives the whole report. The rule-based career simply counts overlap with each career's skill list. It is shown alongside as an easy-to-explain cross-check.

Why does the job match go down when I paste a long job post?

Job match compares the words in both texts. A long post with many topics your resume does not mention lowers the similarity. It is a guide to how closely your wording lines up, not a verdict.

How are applicants ranked for employers?

70% comes from how many of the job's required skills the applicant has, and 30% from how similar their resume and cover letter are to the job description. The highest score is ranked first, and ties share a rank.

Privacy and safety
Where are resumes stored? Who can see them?

Uploaded PDFs are stored privately on the server, not in a public folder. Only the owner can download their own resume, and an employer can only see the resume a seeker attached when applying to that employer's job.

Is resume data sent anywhere else?

No. Analysis happens on the project's own AI service. Nothing is sent to ChatGPT or any other outside AI provider.

Can someone delete their data?

Yes. A seeker can delete any analysis, which also deletes the stored PDF. An admin can delete a whole account, which removes their resumes, applications and saved jobs.

How is the system protected from abuse?

Uploads are limited to 10 per minute per user and 2 MB PDFs. Login and sign-up are limited to 10 attempts per minute per network and 5 per email. Every form is checked on the server, so bad input gets a clear error instead of breaking anything.

The project
What is the system built with?

Three parts: a React website, a Laravel API that handles accounts, jobs and storage, and a Python FastAPI service that runs the AI using scikit-learn and spaCy.

How was it tested?

73 automated backend tests, 21 automated AI tests, and a 45-step end-to-end check that runs every feature against the live system with real PDF resumes. All of them pass.

Where is the code?

On GitHub at github.com/Bisu56/ai-career-system. The AI lives in backend/ai-service, the training script is train_model.py, and the data builder is dataset/build_djinni.py.
